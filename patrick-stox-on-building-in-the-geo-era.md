## Abstract

Patrick Stox argues that SEO is a subset of GEO rather than a rebrand of it: the fastest route to changing what an AI system says about a company is to fix the product, the service and the reputation people are actually complaining about, because that is what the systems repeat. Around that he sets out an operational agenda — close the gap between being retrieved and being cited, replace generated content with genuine information gain harvested from in-house experts, and treat hands-on proof rather than polished prose as the defensible moat after the Helpful Content Update. He also warns that AI search systems have not yet made Google's decision to forgive past manipulation, and that today's scalable tactics carry unpriced future risk.

## Key claims

Each claim is attributed to a named speaker and backed by a verbatim quotation from the transcript. Do not attribute a host statement to the guest, or the reverse.

### 1. SEO is a subset of GEO, not a synonym for it

**Patrick Stox**, in *The Hot Take: SEO Is Part of GEO*:

> If you've got a problem with your product, the easiest way to get the LLM or the AI search system to say something different? Go fix that. Because if people are complaining on the internet, that's what it's gonna say. So it's business, it's branding, it's so much more.

Influencing AI answers is a product, brand and reputation problem before it is an on-page one. Stox draws a scale line: a local business can have an SEO work on sentiment; a large company cannot staff its way to changing the opinion of the internet.

### 2. AI search systems may not forgive the way Google eventually did

**Patrick Stox**, in *Do AI Search Systems Hold a Grudge?*:

> SEOs got lucky with Google. We got Penguin, and for a few years that was really painful — and then they're like, yeah, we forgive all your bad links. These AI search systems may make a different decision. How long will they hold a grudge against you?

Google chose to forgive link spam around 2016. That was a policy decision, not a technical inevitability, and Stox's point is that no one has made that decision on behalf of the AI systems yet. Tactics that work now carry unknown future liability.

### 3. Retrieved is not cited, and the gap is the opportunity

**Patrick Stox**, in *How Google Actually Works*:

> You get a bunch of pages, might be 90 pages retrieved, and then 13 of them get cited... Well, why were some retrieved but not cited? That's a really good question to look into. And it's usually like, you were relevant enough, but you didn't quite say the right thing, or in the right way.

The sharpest operational idea in the episode. Being in the retrieval set is table stakes; citation is a second, separate contest. Stox calls the gap “the low-hanging fruit of the AI era” because the page is already close enough to be pulled.

### 4. The frontier past AI content at scale is information gain

**Patrick Stox**, in *The Frontier Past AI Content at Scale*:

> Anyone can put out any number of pages. But if you're just using it for content — just the content — you're not providing resources, you're not sourcing where you got something. So anyone can do it, but it's a matter of: can you do it well?

His concrete alternative is to harvest expertise you already own: message five genuine experts inside your company via Slack, email or an AI-run interview, two or three times a week, and build content on what they say. He notes he sees almost nobody doing this.

### 5. Do not run a programmatic project without unique data

**Patrick Stox**, in *The Frontier Past AI Content at Scale*:

> Generally I wouldn't even recommend a programmatic project unless you've got something unique, different... Everyone's got reviews, but what if you did scoring? What if you did a market analysis? What's the sentiment? What is the brand search volume for each of these companies?

The differentiator is a derived metric no competitor has computed, not a larger pile of the same rows.

### 6. Hands-on proof is the HCU moat, and writing style is not

**Patrick Stox**, in *HCU, E-E-A-T, and Hands-On Expertise*:

> I don't think writing will get you but so far. Part of the thing they mentioned with that update was images — images and videos that show you've got hands-on with the product you're talking about... Not you pulled the stock photo offline from the manufacturer and then wrote a bunch of stuff.

His examples are competitors cutting running shoes in half to show the materials, and Cats.com sending pet food for laboratory testing. He also argues Google went too far, and that knowledgeable sites were caught unfairly.

### 7. Backlink correlation fell overall but held for local, and scarcity may raise its value

**Patrick Stox**, in *The State of Play in Backlinks*:

> The correlation with rankings went down overall compared to what we've seen traditionally. It was still high for certain things, like local companies... For the AI search systems, they probably are not using this directly, but a lot of them are pulling rankings from Google and Bing, who do still use them.

Links reach AI answers indirectly, through the ranking systems those answers borrow from. Stox adds the counter-intuitive turn: as the industry drifts away from links, rarity could make them a stronger differentiator, not a weaker one.

### 8. Google has more discovery inputs than sitemaps and crawling

**Patrick Stox**, in *How Google Actually Works*:

> Most people will tell you sitemaps, or crawling. Well, there's more. RSS feeds are a thing. Pinging the sites — the WebSub, PubSubHubbub, whatever... Submissions in GSC, the indexing API.

Useful when diagnosing discovery problems: the input set is wider than the two mechanisms most audits check.

### 9. Indie SEO tools can still win, but on niche and speed rather than data

**Patrick Stox**, in *The Two 800-Pound Gorillas and Indie SEO Tools*:

> I think there is plenty of space for more niche tools... They can focus and do things better than the big ones. They can move a little faster at times.

He is candid about the constraint from the other side: he cannot reproduce Ahrefs' crawl infrastructure, so anything he builds will run on smaller scale, weaker data, or uploads and integrations with the tools people already pay for.
